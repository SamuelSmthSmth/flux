### **Question 1**

The point $P(-2, -5)$ lies on the curve with equation $y = f(x)$, $x \in \mathbb{R}$

Find the point to which $P$ is mapped, when the curve with equation $y = f(x)$ is transformed to the curve with equation

**(a)** $y = f(x) + 2$ **(1)**

**(b)** $y = |f(x)|$ **(1)**

**(c)** $y = 3f(x - 2) + 2$ **(2)**

**(Total for Question 1 is 4 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 1**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Apply translation parallel to the $y$-axis**
$(-2, -5 + 2) = (-2, -3)$
- **[B1]** $(-2, -3)$

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 2: Apply modulus function**
$(-2, |-5|) = (-2, 5)$
- **[B1]** $(-2, 5)$

#### **Part (c)**

**Step 3: Apply horizontal translation, vertical stretch, and vertical translation**
$x$-coordinate: $-2 + 2 = 0$
$y$-coordinate: $3(-5) + 2 = -15 + 2 = -13$
So the point is $(0, -13)$
- **[M1]** Correct attempt at two or more of the transformations (e.g., $x$-coordinate is $-2 + 2$, or $y$-coordinate involves $3(-5)$ or $+2$).
- **[A1]** $(0, -13)$

### **Examiner Report 1**

This was an accessible question for virtually all candidates. It asked for the point to which $P$ was mapped, and most candidates gave this as a coordinate pair.

Part (a) was frequently correct; candidates identified the translation and applied it successfully. The attempts at part (b) were less successful. Most solutions used the modulus concept but applied it to both coordinates.

Part (c) was a 3-stage transformation and required the candidates to identify both the transformations and the order in which they were to be applied. This was more of a challenge. In particular, the stretch parallel to the $y$-axis and final translation were mixed up; those who did, generally received some credit as they obtained $x = 0$.

### **Question 2**

$$f(x) = (x - 4)(x^2 - 3x + k) - 42 \quad \text{where } k \text{ is a constant}$$

Given that $(x + 2)$ is a factor of $\mathrm{f}(x)$, find the value of $k$. **(3)**

**(Total for Question 2 is 3 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 2**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Use the factor theorem**
$\mathrm{f}(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow (-2 - 4)((-2)^2 - 3(-2) + k) - 42 = 0$
- **[M1]** Substitutes $x = -2$ into $\mathrm{f}(x)$ and sets the expression equal to $0$.

**Step 2: Simplify the equation**
$(-6)(4 + 6 + k) - 42 = 0$
$-6(10 + k) - 42 = 0$
$-60 - 6k - 42 = 0$
$-102 - 6k = 0$
- **[M1]** Expands and simplifies correctly to form a linear equation in $k$.

**Step 3: Solve for $k$**
$6k = -102 \Rightarrow k = -17$
- **[A1]** $k = -17$

### **Examiner Report 2**

This was a familiar and accessible question with most candidates scoring 2 or 3 marks. The most common and successful approach was via the use of the factor theorem. Following on from setting $\mathrm{f}(-2) = 0$, most candidates were able to set up and solve the linear equation in $k$.

Errors seen resulting in the loss of marks were:
- having $(10k)$ not $(10+k)$ in the linear equation
- expanding $-6(10+k)$ to $\pm 60 \pm k$, or making a sign error on the final stage to get $k = 17$
- setting $\mathrm{f}(2) = 0$
- expanding the expression before substituting, often leading to sign errors or the loss of the $42$

There was a small proportion of candidates who tried to use algebraic division to solve the problem, but most did not score many marks since the working needed here was much more complicated. Often the division was left incomplete, or there was not a linear remainder in $k$ to set equal to zero. Even fewer candidates attempted factorisation by inspection; only a handful of candidates gained marks using this method.

### **Question 3**

A circle has equation

$$x^2 + y^2 - 10x + 16y = 80$$

**(a)** Find 

**(i)** the coordinates of the centre of the circle,

**(ii)** the radius of the circle. **(3)**

Given that $P$ is the point on the circle that is furthest away from the origin $O$,

**(b)** find the exact length $OP$. **(2)**

**(Total for Question 3 is 5 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 3**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Complete the square for $x$ and $y$**
$(x - 5)^2 - 25 + (y + 8)^2 - 64 = 80$
- **[M1]** Completes the square for at least one variable correctly, or uses the standard formula correctly.

**Step 2: State centre and radius**
Centre: $(5, -8)$
Radius: $\sqrt{80 + 25 + 64} = \sqrt{169} = 13$
- **[A1]** Centre is $(5, -8)$
- **[A1]** Radius is $13$

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 3: Calculate the distance from the origin to the centre**
Distance $OC = \sqrt{5^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{25 + 64} = \sqrt{89}$
- **[M1]** Finds the distance from the origin to the centre of the circle.

**Step 4: Calculate the exact length $OP$**
$OP = OC + r = \sqrt{89} + 13$
- **[A1]** $\sqrt{89} + 13$ (or exact equivalent)

### **Examiner Report 3**

This question on the geometry of a circle had standard bookwork in part (a) followed by problem solving in part (b). Success in part (b) was usually achieved by candidates who drew a diagram.

In part (a) most candidates scored all three marks. Some candidates made sign errors when completing the square but still obtained some version of $(\pm 5, \pm 8)$. Many candidates were still able to get the correct radius following this slip. A significant minority of candidates obtained a centre of $(5, -4)$ seemingly from square rooting the $16$ rather than halving.

In part (b), there was full follow through on the centre and radius, which allowed many candidates to score full marks despite errors in part (a). Candidates who drew a diagram worked out that they need to find the distance of the centre from the origin and add on the radius. One lengthy method involved finding the intersection of the circle with the line through $O$ and $C$, but this was unlikely to give the requested exact answer, and some candidates gave up at the point where the quadratic had to be solved as it had unpleasant coefficients. A common error was based on simply doubling the radius, either as $26$ or finding the length of the vector $(10, -16)$ as $2\sqrt{89}$.

### **Question 4**

**(a)** Express $\lim_{\delta x \to 0} \sum_{x=2.1}^{6.3} \frac{2}{x} \delta x$ as an integral. **(1)**

**(b)** Hence show that

$$\lim_{\delta x \to 0} \sum_{x=2.1}^{6.3} \frac{2}{x} \delta x = \ln k$$

where $k$ is a constant to be found. **(2)**

**(Total for Question 4 is 3 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 4**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Write the limit of the sum as a definite integral**
$$\int_{2.1}^{6.3} \frac{2}{x} \, \mathrm{d}x$$
- **[B1]** Correct integral with limits and $\mathrm{d}x$.

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 2: Integrate the function**
$$\int_{2.1}^{6.3} \frac{2}{x} \, \mathrm{d}x = [2 \ln |x|]_{2.1}^{6.3}$$
- **[M1]** Correctly integrates $\frac{2}{x}$ to $2\ln x$ (or equivalent).

**Step 3: Apply limits and simplify**
$$= 2 \ln(6.3) - 2 \ln(2.1) = 2 \left( \ln\left(\frac{6.3}{2.1}\right) \right) = 2 \ln(3) = \ln(3^2) = \ln(9)$$
So $k = 9$.
- **[A1]** Correct working leading to $\ln 9$ (or $\ln 3^2$).

### **Examiner Report 4**

This was a generously worded question with plenty of guidance to help candidates throughout. As a result, it was generally well attempted, with many candidates scoring full marks.

There were a number of candidates who made no attempt at part (a) despite the guidance to express the given sum as an integral. Where candidates had used the information given in the question the correct answer was relatively easy to deduce. Common errors seen in part (a) were omitting the limits or the $\mathrm{d}x$, and writing $\mathrm{d}x$ as $\delta x$ (although this was condoned).

Candidates who had attempted part (a) generally went on to attempt part (b). Most candidates provided a fully correct solution and gained both marks. Where errors were made, this was usually at the last mark. Common errors seen were:
- incorrectly integrating the expression to $\ln x$ or $\ln 2x$
- differentiating $\frac{2}{x}$ rather than integrating it
- leaving the answer as $2\ln 3$ rather than $\ln 9$

### **Question 5**

The height, $h$ metres, of a tree, $t$ years after being planted, is modelled by the equation

$$h^2 = at + b \quad 0 \leqslant t < 25$$

where $a$ and $b$ are constants.

Given that
- the height of the tree was $2.60\text{ m}$, exactly $2\text{ years}$ after being planted
- the height of the tree was $5.10\text{ m}$, exactly $10\text{ years}$ after being planted

**(a)** find a complete equation for the model, giving the values of $a$ and $b$ to $3$ significant figures. **(4)**

Given that the height of the tree was $7\text{ m}$, exactly $20\text{ years}$ after being planted

**(b)** evaluate the model, giving reasons for your answer. **(2)**

**(Total for Question 5 is 6 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 5**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Form simultaneous equations using the given data**
When $t = 2, h = 2.60 \Rightarrow 2.60^2 = 2a + b \Rightarrow 6.76 = 2a + b$
When $t = 10, h = 5.10 \Rightarrow 5.10^2 = 10a + b \Rightarrow 26.01 = 10a + b$
- **[M1]** Substitutes $t$ and $h$ values correctly into the model equation to form two equations.

**Step 2: Solve the simultaneous equations**
$8a = 19.25 \Rightarrow a = 2.40625$
$b = 6.76 - 2(2.40625) = 1.9475$
- **[M1]** Attempts to solve the simultaneous equations to find $a$ and $b$.

**Step 3: Round to 3 significant figures**
$a = 2.41, \quad b = 1.95$
- **[A1]** Correct values for $a$ and $b$ to 3 s.f. ($a = 2.41, b = 1.95$).

**Step 4: State the complete equation**
$h^2 = 2.41t + 1.95$
- **[A1]** States the complete equation explicitly using the rounded values.

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 5: Test the model when $t = 20$**
When $t = 20$, $h^2 = 2.40625(20) + 1.9475 = 50.0725 \Rightarrow h = \sqrt{50.0725} \approx 7.08\text{ m}$
(Or using 3 s.f. values: $h^2 = 2.41(20) + 1.95 = 50.15 \Rightarrow h = \sqrt{50.15} \approx 7.08\text{ m}$)
- **[M1]** Substitutes $t = 20$ into their model and finds the corresponding value of $h$ (or substitutes $h = 7$ to find $t$).

**Step 6: Evaluate the model**
The model gives a height of approximately $7.08\text{ m}$, which is very close to the actual height of $7\text{ m}$ (or percentage error $\approx 1.1\%$), so the model is a good fit / works well.
- **[A1]** Evaluates the model with a valid reason (e.g., close to $7\text{ m}$, or gives a correct comparison/percentage error).

### **Examiner Report 5**

This question involved solving simultaneous equations set within the context of the height of a tree modelled over a number of years. The question was very accessible for many candidates.

In part (a) the majority of candidates gained the first three marks for forming and solving two correct simultaneous equations. Many went to a great deal of effort to solve the simultaneous equations using algebraic methods, whereas some just put the equations into a calculator and wrote down the values of the constants $a$ and $b$. Some candidates used the 3 significant figure value of $a$ to subsequently find the value of $b$, resulting in finding $b$ as $1.94$ rather than $1.95$ and thus losing the final A mark. A significant number of candidates correctly found the values of the constants $a$ and $b$, but then failed to state the equation of the model explicitly anywhere in their answer, losing the final A mark.

In part (b) most candidates realised they had to substitute either $t = 20$ or $h = 7$ into their model and compare the value obtained with the given values. However, some candidates did not know how to evaluate the model with several coming to the wrong conclusion that the model was poor, because it didn’t give the exact values for $h$ or $t$. There were quite a few candidates who correctly calculated the percentage error as an impressive comparison. It is very important for candidates to give a reason for their answer when the question explicitly demands it.

### **Question 6**

**Figure 1** _(A sketch of a curve $C$ with equation $y = \mathrm{f}(x)$ where $\mathrm{f}(x)$ is a cubic expression in $x$, passing through the origin, with a maximum turning point at $(2, 8)$ and a minimum turning point at $(6, 0)$)._

```tikz
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
  \draw[->] (-1,0) -- (8,0) node[below] {$x$};
  \draw[->] (0,-2) -- (0,10) node[left] {$y$};
  \draw[domain=-0.5:6.8, smooth, thick] plot (\x, {(\x)*(\x)*(\x)/6 - 2*(\x)*(\x) + 6*(\x)});
  \fill (2,8) circle (1.6pt) node[above] {$(2, 8)$};
  \fill (6,0) circle (1.6pt) node[below right] {$6$};
  \draw (0,0) node[below left] {$O$};
  \node[above right] at (6.5,7) {$C$};
\end{tikzpicture}
```

Figure 1 shows a sketch of a curve $C$ with equation $y = \mathrm{f}(x)$ where $\mathrm{f}(x)$ is a cubic expression in $x$.

The curve
- passes through the origin
- has a maximum turning point at $(2, 8)$
- has a minimum turning point at $(6, 0)$

**(a)** Write down the set of values of $x$ for which $\mathrm{f}'(x) < 0$. **(1)**

The line with equation $y = k$, where $k$ is a constant, intersects $C$ at only one point.

**(b)** Find the set of values of $k$, giving your answer in set notation. **(2)**

**(c)** Find the equation of $C$. You may leave your answer in factorised form. **(3)**

**(Total for Question 6 is 6 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 6**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Identify the range where the function is decreasing**
$2 < x < 6$
- **[B1]** $2 < x < 6$ (allow equivalent notations such as $(2, 6)$).

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 2: Determine the range of $k$ for one intersection**
$k < 0$ or $k > 8$
- **[M1]** Identifies at least one of the correct boundary conditions ($k < 0$ or $k > 8$).
- **[A1]** $\{k \in \mathbb{R} : k < 0\} \cup \{k \in \mathbb{R} : k > 8\}$ (or $\{k : k < 0\} \cup \{k : k > 8\}$ or $(-\infty, 0) \cup (8, \infty)$).

#### **Part (c)**

**Step 3: Set up the equation of the cubic curve using the turning points/roots**
Since $(6, 0)$ is a minimum turning point, $x = 6$ is a repeated root:
$y = a(x - 6)^2(x - p)$
Using the other turning point at $x = 2$: when $x = 2, y = 8$:
$8 = a(2 - 6)^2(2 - p) \Rightarrow 8 = 16a(2 - p)$
Alternatively, using derivative: $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = a'(x - 2)(x - 6) = a'(x^2 - 8x + 12)$
$\Rightarrow y = a'\left(\frac{x^3}{3} - 4x^2 + 12x\right) + c$
Using $(0, 0) \Rightarrow c = 0$. Using $(2, 8)$:
$8 = a'\left(\frac{8}{3} - 16 + 24\right) = a'\left(\frac{32}{3}\right) \Rightarrow a' = \frac{24}{32} = \frac{3}{4}$.
So $y = \frac{1}{4}x(x - 6)^2$ (or equivalent).
- **[M1]** Sets up a valid form for the curve, e.g., $y = a(x - 6)^2(x - p)$ or $y = ax(x - 6)^2$ or $\int a(x - 2)(x - 6) \, \mathrm{d}x$.
- **[M1]** Uses coordinates $(2, 8)$ (or another valid point/condition) to find the stretch factor $a$.
- **[A1]** $y = \frac{1}{4}x(x - 6)^2$ (or equivalent factorised form).

### **Examiner Report 6**

There was a very mixed response to this question on a cubic function with only one in eight candidates scoring all 6 marks.

Part (a) was for finding the set of values of $x$ for which $\mathrm{f}'(x) < 0$. Candidates generally knew what to do and most scored this B mark. Marks were lost when candidates gave the inequality as $2 \leqslant x \leqslant 6$ or else $2 < \mathrm{f}(x) < 6$.

In part (b) the request to give the answer in set notation proved a challenge for many candidates, with many only gaining the M mark for correctly identifying at least one of the correct inequalities. A minority of candidates gained both marks by giving their answer in acceptable set notation but there was widespread misapplication of $\cap$ rather than $\cup$.

Part (c) required the candidates to find the equation of the curve. A very common response, which gained only the first mark, was to give their answer as $y = x(x - 6)^2$ without any appreciation of the stretch factor. Candidates who did appreciate that it could be $y = ax(x - 6)^2$ could easily use the coordinate $(2, 8)$ to find the value of $a$ and hence the equation of the curve. A sizeable minority of otherwise good candidates lost the final mark by incorrectly writing down the equation of the curve as $\frac{1}{4}x(x - 6)^2 = C$. There were many more time consuming and long winded methods seen here. A significant number of candidates formed the equation of a cubic, i.e., $y = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d$ and then attempted to use the points on the curve to find the values of $a$, $b$, $c$ and $d$. This approach was rarely fully correct with many failing to identify that $d = 0$ or being able to form only 2 equations. A small minority of candidates did attempt to use the turning points at $x = 2$ and $x = 6$ to form the equation $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = (x - 2)(x - 6)$ without again, an appreciation of the need for a stretch factor. It was disappointing to see how many candidates got bogged down in pages of algebraic working without noticing that this part of the question was only worth 3 marks.

### **Question 7**

**(i)** Given that $p$ and $q$ are integers such that

$$pq \text{ is even}$$

use algebra to prove by contradiction that at least one of $p$ or $q$ is even. **(3)**

**(ii)** Given that $x$ and $y$ are integers such that
- $x < 0$
- $(x + y)^2 < 9x^2 + y^2$

show that $y > 4x$. **(2)**

**(Total for Question 7 is 5 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 7**

#### **Part (i)**

**Step 1: State the assumption for proof by contradiction**
Assume that neither $p$ nor $q$ is even (i.e., both $p$ and $q$ are odd).
- **[B1]** Assumes both $p$ and $q$ are odd (with appropriate definitions such as $p = 2a + 1, q = 2b + 1$).

**Step 2: Multiply the two odd numbers**
Let $p = 2a + 1$ and $q = 2b + 1$ where $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$.
$pq = (2a + 1)(2b + 1) = 4ab + 2a + 2b + 1 = 2(2ab + a + b) + 1$
- **[M1]** Correctly expresses the product of two odd numbers and shows it is of the form $2k + 1$ (odd).

**Step 3: State the contradiction and conclusion**
This shows $pq$ is odd, which contradicts the given statement that $pq$ is even. Therefore, at least one of $p$ or $q$ must be even.
- **[A1]** Complete and rigorous proof with a clear concluding statement.

#### **Part (ii)**

**Step 4: Expand the inequality**
$x^2 + 2xy + y^2 < 9x^2 + y^2$
$2xy < 8x^2$
- **[M1]** Expands $(x + y)^2$ and correctly simplifies the inequality to $2xy < 8x^2$ (or equivalent).

**Step 5: Divide by $2x$ taking account of the inequality sign ($x < 0$)**
Since $x < 0$, dividing by $2x$ reverses the inequality sign:
$y > 4x$
- **[A1]** Reaches $y > 4x$ with clear justification regarding division by a negative number.

### **Examiner Report 7**

This question, on two different kinds of proof, proved more demanding than expected. As a result, there were very few completely correct solutions to both parts of this question.

In part (i) candidates were often unable to set up a proof by contradiction using appropriate language, failing to use words such as 'assume', 'there exists' or 'let'. Others simply set up a contradiction such as 'assume $p$ and $q$ are odd' without any reference to $pq$ being even. Many candidates also failed to use different variables for their odd numbers $p$ and $q$, implying that $p$ and $q$ were either the same odd number, or two consecutive odd numbers such as $2n+1$ and $2n+3$. Candidates who managed to write $p$ and $q$ using different variables were generally able to multiply out $pq$ correctly and write it in the form $2(\dots)+1$. If this stage was reached, the conclusion was generally correct and an acceptable sentence was written down. A mark of $011$ was possible from the mark scheme.

In part (ii) most candidates expanded the left hand side and cancelled terms getting to $2xy < 8x^2$ or equivalent for the method mark. Many then lost the second mark by failing to identify the point at which the inequality reverses, simply writing down $y < 4x$ followed by 'so $y > 4x$'. Other candidates did reverse the inequality at the right point but didn't give an explanation as to why, and so lost the final mark.

### **Question 8**

**Figure 2** _(A sketch of the speed-time graph $v = (10 - 0.4t)\ln(t + 1)$ for a car travelling between two sets of traffic lights from $t = 0$ to $t = T$)._

```tikz
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9]
  \draw[->] (-0.5,0) -- (6,0) node[below] {$t$};
  \draw[->] (0,-0.5) -- (0,4) node[left] {$v$};
  \draw[domain=0:5.2, smooth, thick] plot (\x, {(3 - 0.2*\x)*ln(\x + 1)});
  \draw (5.2,0) node[below] {$T$};
  \fill (5.2,0) circle (1.5pt);
  \draw (0,0) node[below left] {$O$};
\end{tikzpicture}
```

A car stops at two sets of traffic lights.

Figure 2 shows a graph of the speed of the car, $v\text{ m s}^{-1}$, as it travels between the two sets of traffic lights.

The car takes $T$ seconds to travel between the two sets of traffic lights.

The speed of the car is modelled by the equation

$$v = (10 - 0.4t)\ln(t + 1) \quad 0 \leqslant t \leqslant T$$

where $t$ seconds is the time after the car leaves the first set of traffic lights.

According to the model,

**(a)** find the value of $T$. **(1)**

**(b)** show that the maximum speed of the car occurs when

$$t = \frac{26}{1 + \ln(t + 1)} - 1$$

Using the iteration formula

$$t_{n+1} = \frac{26}{1 + \ln(t_n + 1)} - 1$$

with $t_1 = 7$

**(c)** 

**(i)** find the value of $t_3$ to $3$ decimal places,

**(ii)** find, by repeated iteration, the time taken for the car to reach maximum speed. **(3)**

**(Total for Question 8 is 8 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 8**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Find $T$ where $v = 0$**
$(10 - 0.4T)\ln(T + 1) = 0$
Since $T > 0$, $\ln(T + 1) \neq 0$ for $T > 0$, so $10 - 0.4T = 0 \Rightarrow T = 25$
- **[B1]** $T = 25$

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 2: Differentiate $v$ with respect to $t$ using the product rule**
Let $u = 10 - 0.4t \Rightarrow u' = -0.4$
Let $v = \ln(t + 1) \Rightarrow v' = \frac{1}{t + 1}$
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = (10 - 0.4t)\left(\frac{1}{t + 1}\right) - 0.4\ln(t + 1)$$
- **[M1]** Uses the product rule correctly to differentiate $v$.
- **[A1]** Correct derivative expression.

**Step 3: Set derivative to zero and rearrange**
For maximum speed, $\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0$:
$$\frac{10 - 0.4t}{t + 1} - 0.4\ln(t + 1) = 0$$
$$\frac{10 - 0.4t}{t + 1} = 0.4\ln(t + 1)$$
Divide by $0.4$:
$$\frac{25 - t}{t + 1} = \ln(t + 1)$$
$$\frac{26 - t - 1}{t + 1} = \ln(t + 1) \implies \frac{26}{t + 1} - 1 = \ln(t + 1)$$
$$\ln(t + 1) + 1 = \frac{26}{t + 1}$$
$$t + 1 = \frac{26}{1 + \ln(t + 1)}$$
$$t = \frac{26}{1 + \ln(t + 1)} - 1$$
- **[M1]** Sets $\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0$ and rearranges algebraically towards the given form.
- **[A1]** Fully correct algebraic manipulation leading to the required result.

#### **Part (c)**

**Step 4: Perform the iteration**
$t_1 = 7$
$t_2 = \frac{26}{1 + \ln(8)} - 1 = \frac{26}{1 + 2.07944} - 1 = \frac{26}{3.07944} - 1 = 7.4426$
$t_3 = \frac{26}{1 + \ln(8.4426)} - 1 = 7.3751$
To 3 d.p.: $t_3 = 7.375$
- **[M1]** Correct substitution to find $t_2$ and $t_3$.
- **[A1]** $t_3 = 7.375$ (to 3 d.p.)

**Step 5: Continue iteration to find limit**
$t_4 = 7.386$
$t_5 = 7.384$
$t_6 = 7.385$
Time taken $= 7.38\text{ seconds}$ (or $7.39\text{ seconds}$ depending on rounding, usually $7.38$ or $7.39$).
- **[B1]** $7.38$ or $7.39$ seconds (accept anything that rounds to $7.38$ or $7.39$ with units).

### **Examiner Report 8**

This was the second modelling question on the paper. The given equation modelled the speed of a car as it travelled between a pair of traffic lights. Many weaker candidates missed out part (b) and scored marks in the more accessible parts (a) and (c).

Most candidates managed to reach the correct answer of $25$ in part (a). A common incorrect answer was $10$, possibly from sight of the $10$ in the given equation $v = (10 - 0.4t)\ln(t + 1)$.

The first two marks in part (b) were often achieved by those who attempted it. Using the product rule to find $\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}$ and then setting this $= 0$ gave them one form of the given equation. The final two marks were only achieved by the best of students. In reality, all that was required was to make $t$ the subject of the formula, but it was beyond most.

Part (c) was the most successful part of the question and familiar to most candidates. Many were able to carry out the iteration process correctly, and write down $7.298$ [Note: specific student numbers varied] without any problems. In part (c)(ii), the omission of the units, “seconds”, was the most common slip.

### **Question 9**

**Figure 3** _(A sketch of a parallelogram $PQRS$)._

```tikz

```

Figure 3 shows a sketch of a parallelogram $PQRS$.

Given that
- $\vec{PQ} = 2\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j} - 4\mathbf{k}$
- $\vec{QR} = 5\mathbf{i} - 2\mathbf{k}$

**(a)** show that parallelogram $PQRS$ is a rhombus. **(2)**

**(b)** Find the exact area of the rhombus $PQRS$. **(4)**

**(Total for Question 9 is 6 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 9**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Find the magnitude of adjacent sides $\vec{PQ}$ and $\vec{QR}$**
$|\vec{PQ}| = \sqrt{2^2 + 3^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 16} = \sqrt{29}$
$|\vec{QR}| = \sqrt{5^2 + 0^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{25 + 0 + 4} = \sqrt{29}$
- **[M1]** Calculates the magnitude of $\vec{PQ}$ and $\vec{QR}$.
- **[A1]** Concludes that since adjacent sides are equal in length ($|\vec{PQ}| = |\vec{QR}| = \sqrt{29}$), the parallelogram is a rhombus.

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 2: Find the angle between the vectors or use vector cross product**
Method 1 (Using scalar product):
$\vec{PQ} \cdot \vec{QR} = (2)(5) + (3)(0) + (-4)(-2) = 10 + 0 + 8 = 18$
$\cos \theta = \frac{\vec{PQ} \cdot \vec{QR}}{|\vec{PQ}||\vec{QR}|} = \frac{18}{\sqrt{29}\sqrt{29}} = \frac{18}{29}$
$\sin \theta = \sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{18}{29}\right)^2} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{324}{841}} = \sqrt{\frac{517}{841}} = \frac{\sqrt{517}}{29}$

**Step 3: Calculate the area of the rhombus**
$\text{Area} = |\vec{PQ}||\vec{QR}|\sin \theta = \sqrt{29}\sqrt{29}\left(\frac{\sqrt{517}}{29}\right) = \sqrt{517}$
(Alternatively, using cross product: $\vec{PQ} \times \vec{QR} = (3(-2) - (-4)(0))\mathbf{i} + ((-4)(5) - (2)(-2))\mathbf{j} + (2(0) - 3(5))\mathbf{k} = -6\mathbf{i} - 16\mathbf{j} - 15\mathbf{k}$, magnitude $= \sqrt{(-6)^2 + (-16)^2 + (-15)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 256 + 225} = \sqrt{517}$).
- **[M1]** Attempts to find the angle between vectors (or uses vector cross product / diagonals method).
- **[M1]** Correct method for area (e.g., $|\vec{PQ}||\vec{QR}|\sin\theta$ or $|\vec{PQ} \times \vec{QR}|$ or $\frac{1}{2}|\vec{PR}||\vec{QS}|$).
- **[A1]** $\sqrt{517}$ (exact area).

### **Examiner Report 9**

Although the requirement to 'show' is becoming more familiar to candidates, a significant number in this case didn't recognise the level of detail expected. It was also necessary to conclude that the sides were equal, and not simply find the lengths and leave it to the reader to decide.

A vast array of approaches were in evidence in part (b) to find the area of the rhombus. The easiest and most direct approach was to find $|\vec{PQ} \times \vec{QR}|$. ### **Question 10**

A scientist is studying the number of bees and the number of wasps on an island.

The number of bees, measured in thousands, $N_b$, is modelled by the equation

$$N_b = 45 + 220\mathrm{e}^{0.05t}$$

where $t$ is the number of years from the start of the study.

According to the model,

**(a)** find the number of bees at the start of the study, **(1)**

**(b)** show that, exactly $10\text{ years}$ after the start of the study, the number of bees was increasing at a rate of approximately $18\text{ thousand per year}$. **(3)**

The number of wasps, measured in thousands, $N_w$, is modelled by the equation

$$N_w = 10 + 800\mathrm{e}^{-0.05t}$$

where $t$ is the number of years from the start of the study.

When $t = T$, according to the models, there are an equal number of bees and wasps.

**(c)** Find the value of $T$ to $2$ decimal places. **(4)**

**(Total for Question 10 is 8 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 10**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Evaluate $N_b$ at $t = 0$**
When $t = 0$, $N_b = 45 + 220\mathrm{e}^0 = 45 + 220 = 265$ (thousand) $\Rightarrow 265,000$
- **[B1]** $265,000$ (or $265$ thousand).

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 2: Differentiate $N_b$ with respect to $t$**
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}N_b}{\mathrm{d}t} = 220(0.05)\mathrm{e}^{0.05t} = 11\mathrm{e}^{0.05t}$$
- **[M1]** Differentiates $N_b$ correctly to get $k\mathrm{e}^{0.05t}$ (with $k = 11$).

**Step 3: Evaluate the derivative at $t = 10$**
When $t = 10$:
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}N_b}{\mathrm{d}t} = 11\mathrm{e}^{0.05(10)} = 11\mathrm{e}^{0.5} \approx 18.147$$
Since $18.147 \approx 18$, the number of bees was increasing at approximately $18\text{ thousand per year}$.
- **[A1]** Calculates $18.147...$ (or states $11\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}$).
- **[A1]** Concludes with a brief statement linking to approximately 18 thousand per year.

#### **Part (c)**

**Step 4: Set the two equations equal to each other**
$$45 + 220\mathrm{e}^{0.05T} = 10 + 800\mathrm{e}^{-0.05T}$$
- **[M1]** Equates the two expressions for bees and wasps.

**Step 5: Form a quadratic equation in terms of $\mathrm{e}^{0.05T}$**
Multiply by $\mathrm{e}^{0.05T}$:
$$35 + 220\mathrm{e}^{0.05T} = 800\mathrm{e}^{-0.05T}\mathrm{e}^{0.05T} = 800 \quad (\text{Wait: } (45 - 10 = 35))$$
Let $x = \mathrm{e}^{0.05T}$:
$220x + 35 = 800x^{-1} \Rightarrow 220x^2 + 35x - 800 = 0$
- **[M1]** Rearranges into a quadratic equation in $\mathrm{e}^{0.05T}$ (or $\mathrm{e}^{-0.05T}$).

**Step 6: Solve the quadratic equation**
$44x^2 + 7x - 160 = 0$
Using quadratic formula:
$x = \frac{-7 \pm \sqrt{7^2 - 4(44)(-160)}}{2(44)} = \frac{-7 \pm \sqrt{49 + 28160}}{88} = \frac{-7 \pm \sqrt{28209}}{88} = \frac{-7 \pm 167.955}{88}$
Taking the positive root:
$x = \frac{160.955}{88} \approx 1.829$
- **[M1]** Solves the quadratic to find a positive value for $\mathrm{e}^{0.05T}$.

**Step 7: Solve for $T$**
$\mathrm{e}^{0.05T} = 1.82903$
$0.05T = \ln(1.82903) \approx 0.60377$
$T = \frac{0.60377}{0.05} \approx 12.08$

### **Question 12**

In this question you must show all stages of your working.

Solutions relying on calculator technology are not acceptable.

Show that

$$\int_{1}^{\mathrm{e}^2} x^3 \ln x \, \mathrm{d}x = a\mathrm{e}^8 + b$$

where $a$ and $b$ are rational constants to be found. **(5)**

**(Total for Question 12 is 5 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 12**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Set up integration by parts**
Let $u = \ln x \Rightarrow \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x} = \frac{1}{x}$
Let $\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} = x^3 \Rightarrow v = \frac{x^4}{4}$
- **[M1]** Correct choice of $u$ and $\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}$ and correct application of the integration by parts formula.

**Step 2: Apply the integration by parts formula**
$$\int x^3 \ln x \, \mathrm{d}x = \frac{x^4}{4}\ln x - \int \frac{x^4}{4} \left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \, \mathrm{d}x$$
$$= \frac{x^4}{4}\ln x - \int \frac{x^3}{4} \, \mathrm{d}x$$
- **[A1]** Correct expression after integration by parts: $\frac{x^4}{4}\ln x - \int \frac{x^3}{4} \, \mathrm{d}x$.

**Step 3: Integrate the remaining term**
$$= \frac{x^4}{4}\ln x - \frac{x^4}{16}$$
- **[A1]** Correct integrated expression: $\frac{x^4}{4}\ln x - \frac{x^4}{16}$.

**Step 4: Substitute the limits $\mathrm{e}^2$ and $1$**
$$\left[ \frac{x^4}{4}\ln x - \frac{x^4}{16} \right]_{1}^{\mathrm{e}^2} = \left( \frac{(\mathrm{e}^2)^4}{4}\ln(\mathrm{e}^2) - \frac{(\mathrm{e}^2)^4}{16} \right) - \left( \frac{1^4}{4}\ln(1) - \frac{1^4}{16} \right)$$
$$= \left( \frac{\mathrm{e}^8}{4}(2) - \frac{\mathrm{e}^8}{16} \right) - \left( 0 - \frac{1}{16} \right)$$
- **[M1]** Substitutes the limits $\mathrm{e}^2$ and $1$ correctly into their integrated expression (recognising $\ln(\mathrm{e}^2) = 2$ and $\ln(1) = 0$).

**Step 5: Simplify to the required form**
$$= \left( \frac{2\mathrm{e}^8}{4} - \frac{\mathrm{e}^8}{16} \right) + \frac{1}{16} = \left( \frac{8\mathrm{e}^8}{16} - \frac{\mathrm{e}^8}{16} \right) + \frac{1}{16} = \frac{7}{16}\mathrm{e}^8 + \frac{1}{16}$$
So $a = \frac{7}{16}$ and $b = \frac{1}{16}$.
- **[A1]** $\frac{7}{16}\mathrm{e}^8 + \frac{1}{16}$ (or exact correct rational values for $a$ and $b$).

### **Examiner Report 12**

### **Question 13**

Show that

**(3)**

**(ii)** James saves money over a number of weeks to buy a printer that costs $£64$.

Given that James takes $n$ weeks to save exactly $£64$

**(a)** show that

$$n^2 - 26n + 160 = 0$$

**(2)**

**(b)** Solve the equation

**(1)**

**(c)** Hence state the number of weeks James takes to save enough money to buy the printer, giving a brief reason for your answer. **(1)**

**(Total for Question 13 is 7 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 13**

#### **Part (i)**

**Step 1: Write out the sum of the arithmetic series in full**
$S_n = a + (a + d) + (a + 2d) + \dots + (a + (n - 1)d)$
- **[B1]** States the sum of an arithmetic series correctly with first term $a$ and last term $l = a + (n-1)d$.

**Step 2: Write the sum in reverse and add**
$S_n = a + (a + d) + \dots + (l - d) + l$
$S_n = l + (l - d) + \dots + (a + d) + a$
$2S_n = (a + l) + (a + l) + \dots + (a + l) = n(a + l)$
- **[M1]** Demonstrates the standard method for proving the sum formula by writing the sum forwards and backwards and adding.

**Step 3: Substitute $l = a + (n - 1)d$ and divide by 2**
$2S_n = n(a + a + (n - 1)d) = n[2a + (n - 1)d] \Rightarrow S_n = \frac{n}{2}[2a + (n - 1)d]$
- **[A1]** Completes the proof rigorously to arrive at the required formula.

#### **Part (ii)(a)**

**Step 4: Set up the sum equation with given values**
$a = 10, d = -0.8, S_n = 64$
$\frac{n}{2}[2(10) + (n - 1)(-0.8)] = 64$
- **[M1]** Substitutes $a = 10$, $d = -0.8$ and $S_n = 64$ into the arithmetic series sum formula.

**Step 5: Simplify and rearrange to form the quadratic equation**
$\frac{n}{2}[20 - 0.8n + 0.8] = 64$
$\frac{n}{2}[20.8 - 0.8n] = 64$
$n(10.4 - 0.4n) = 64$
$10.4n - 0.4n^2 = 64$
Divide by $-0.4$:
$n^2 - 26n + 160 = 0$
- **[A1]** Correct algebraic manipulation leading to the given quadratic equation.

#### **Part (ii)(b)**

**Step 6: Solve the quadratic equation**
$(n - 10)(n - 16) = 0 \Rightarrow n = 10, n = 16$
- **[B1]** Both values found: $n = 10$ and $n = 16$.

#### **Part (ii)(c)**

**Step 7: Select the correct number of weeks with reason**
$n = 10$ weeks. Reason: After 10 weeks he has saved $£64$ and subsequent terms are negative (or after 16 weeks he would have started saving negative amounts / total savings decrease / 10 is the first time he reaches $£64$).
- **[B1]** $10$ weeks with a valid reason (e.g., $10$ is the first time total savings reach $£64$, or at $16$ weeks savings start decreasing/negative).

**Step 4: Convert to $\tan x$ and conclude**
$\frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} \times 2 \Rightarrow \tan x = 3\sqrt{3}$
- **[A1]** Fully correct proof reaching $\tan x = 3\sqrt{3}$ with no errors.

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 5: Relate part (b) to part (a)**
Using the result from part (a), replacing $x$ with $2\theta$:
$\tan 2\theta = 3\sqrt{3}$
- **[M1]** Deduces that $\tan 2\theta = 3\sqrt{3}$ using the 'hence' link.

**Step 6: Find the principal value and other solutions for $2\theta$**
$2\theta = \arctan(3\sqrt{3}) \approx 79.106^\circ$
Since $0 \leqslant \theta < 180^\circ$, the range for $2\theta$ is $0 \leqslant 2\theta < 360^\circ$.
Other solution: $2\theta = 79.106^\circ + 180^\circ = 259.106^\circ$.
- **[M1]** Correctly finds at least one value for $2\theta$ (e.g., $79.1^\circ$ or $259.1^\circ$).

**Step 7: Solve for $\theta$**
$\theta = \frac{79.106^\circ}{2} \approx 39.6^\circ$
$\theta = \frac{259.106^\circ}{2} \approx 130.6^\circ$
- **[A1]** $\theta = 39.6^\circ$ (to 1 d.p.).
- **[A1]** $\theta = 130.6^\circ$ (to 1 d.p.).

### **Examiner Report 14**

This question was another big discriminator. Good candidates found it familiar and accessible with some $25\%$ scoring full marks. Strangely, for candidates who dropped marks, they seemed to be able to do either part (a) or part (b), but not both.

For those candidates who attempted part (a) most recognised the need to use the compound angle expansions. Carelessness with the multiplication by $2$, signs and use of the formulas was seen in a small minority of the scripts. Students who attempted to use $\sin(A+B)$ and $\cos(A+B)$ with a negative angle $B$, often introduced sign errors; it is clear that such a method is not to be recommended. Substituting exact numerical values was usually correct, however candidates occasionally fudged their working to get the given answer.

Part (b) could be attempted without having done part (a) and many took up this opportunity. Those who paid attention to the 'hence', deducing that $x = 2\theta + 60$, were very successful in achieving the correct answers, though quite a few only found one solution. For the many students who did not see the link between parts (a) and (b), generally encountered much more complex, longer algebraic manipulation. Such students re-started the question using the compound angle formulae with a combination of other trigonometric identities, but were often not able to reach an equation in one function, or else made numerous slips. If quadratics in $\tan \theta$ were reached, they were dealt with competently. If the candidate’s efforts led to $\tan 2\theta = k$, $k \neq 3\sqrt{3}$ or $\tan(2\theta \pm 60) = 3\sqrt{3}$ they were generally able to use the correct order of operations to solve their equation to find at least one solution.

A fair number of correct answers were seen written down without any correct working. As the question had a warning about the use of calculator technology, these responses gained no marks.

### **Question 15**

**Figure 5** _(A sketch of a solid toy shaped like a piece of cheese, with sector face $ABC$ of radius $r$ and angle $0.8\text{ rad}$, and parallel congruent face $DEF$, connected by rectangular faces of length $h$)._

A company makes toys for children.

Figure 5 shows the design for a solid toy that looks like a piece of cheese.

The toy is modelled so that
- face $ABC$ is a sector of a circle with radius $r\text{ cm}$ and centre $A$
- angle $ABC = 0.8\text{ radians}$ (Wait: angle $BAC = 0.8\text{ radians}$ according to question text)
- faces $ABC$ and $DEF$ are congruent
- edges $AD$, $CF$ and $BE$ are perpendicular to faces $ABC$ and $DEF$
- edges $AD$, $CF$ and $BE$ have length $h\text{ cm}$

Given that the volume of the toy is $240\text{ cm}^3$

**(a)** show that the surface area of the toy, $S\text{ cm}^2$, is given by

$$S = 0.8r^2 + \frac{1680}{r}$$

making your method clear. **(4)**

Using algebraic differentiation,

**(b)** find the value of $r$ for which $S$ has a stationary point. **(4)**

**(c)** Prove, by further differentiation, that this value of $r$ gives the minimum surface area of the toy. **(2)**

**(Total for Question 15 is 10 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 15**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Set up the volume formula**
Volume $V = \text{Area of cross section } \times \text{length } h$
Area of sector $ABC = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}r^2(0.8) = 0.4r^2$
Volume $= 0.4r^2h = 240$
- **[M1]** Correct expression for the volume of the toy equated to $240$.

**Step 2: Express $h$ in terms of $r$**
$h = \frac{240}{0.4r^2} = \frac{600}{r^2}$
- **[A1]** $h = \frac{600}{r^2}$ (or equivalent).

**Step 3: Set up the surface area formula**
Surface area $S = 2(\text{Area of sector } ABC) + \text{Area of rectangular faces}$
$S = 2(0.4r^2) + r(0.8)h + 2rh = 0.8r^2 + 0.8rh + 2rh = 0.8r^2 + 2.8rh$
(Wait, perimeter of sector is $r + r + r\theta = 2r + 0.8r = 2.8r$. So rectangular faces have area $(2.8r)h$).
$S = 0.8r^2 + 2.8rh$
- **[M1]** Correct expression for total surface area containing sector areas and rectangular side areas.

**Step 4: Substitute $h$ into $S$ and simplify**
$S = 0.8r^2 + 2.8r\left(\frac{600}{r^2}\right) = 0.8r^2 + \frac{1680}{r}$
- **[A1]** Fully correct substitution and simplification to reach the given result.

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 5: Differentiate $S$ with respect to $r$**
$S = 0.8r^2 + 1680r^{-1}$
$\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}r} = 1.6r - 1680r^{-2} = 1.6r - \frac{1680}{r^2}$
- **[M1]** Attempts to differentiate $S$ with respect to $r$ (at least one term correct).
- **[A1]** Completely correct derivative: $1.6r - \frac{1680}{r^2}$.

**Step 6: Set derivative to zero and solve for $r$**
$1.6r - \frac{1680}{r^2} = 0 \Rightarrow 1.6r = \frac{1680}{r^2}$
$1.6r^3 = 1680 \Rightarrow r^3 = \frac{1680}{1.6} = 1050$
$r = \sqrt[3]{1050} \approx 10.16...$ (or $10.2$)
- **[M1]** Sets $\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}r} = 0$ and rearranges to solve for $r^3$ or $r$.
- **[A1]** $r = \sqrt[3]{1050}$ (or $10.2$).

#### **Part (c)**

**Step 7: Find the second derivative and test**
$\frac{\mathrm{d}^2S}{\mathrm{d}r^2} = 1.6 + 3360r^{-3} = 1.6 + \frac{3360}{r^3}$
- **[M1]** Finds the second derivative $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2S}{\mathrm{d}r^2}$.

**Step 8: Evaluate at $r = \sqrt[3]{1050}$ and conclude**
At $r = \sqrt[3]{1050}$, $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2S}{\mathrm{d}r^2} = 1.6 + \frac{3360}{1050} = 1.6 + 3.2 = 4.8 > 0$.
Since $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2S}{\mathrm{d}r^2} > 0$, this value of $r$ gives a minimum surface area.
- **[A1]** Evaluates second derivative (or shows $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}r$ changes sign) and concludes it is a minimum.

### **Examiner Report 15**

This question, based on the minimum surface area of a children's toy proved to be a useful source of marks so late in the paper. In fact $25\%$ of the cohort achieved full marks. Many candidates who could not access part (a) were able to use their skills in differentiation to find an appropriate value for the radius and prove that their value gave a minimum surface area.

Unsurprisingly, part (a) was the most demanding part of this question. The key was to find an expression (using the volume of $240\text{ cm}^3$) for $h$ in terms of $r$. Unfortunately some solutions featured the volume of a sphere, which proved a costly error, from which there was no return. Candidates produced their formula for surface area in a variety of ways – sometimes leaving it to the last line to substitute in the value for the angle, occasionally replacing $0.8$ by $4/5$. As with other “show” questions, sufficient detail has to be written to convince the reader that progress is genuine.

Part (b) was generally well done. Many candidates scored full marks for reaching either $\sqrt[3]{1050}$ or $10.2$. Occasionally some candidates mistakenly wrote down $\sqrt{1050}$ and subsequently simplified their answer to $5\sqrt{42}$. Very few candidates who attempted this part failed to score marks. The main reason for this was usually an inability to differentiate $\frac{1680}{r}$ which usually became $-\frac{1680}{1}$ or $1680\ln r$.

Most candidates were able to find the second derivative correctly in part (c). How it should be used was confused in much of the work seen. Common reasons for a loss of marks here were:
- candidates solving the equation $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2S}{\mathrm{d}r^2} = 0$
- use of incorrect notation including $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2S}{\mathrm{d}r^2}$ and $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}$
- incomplete solutions with calculations or conclusions missing

### **Question 16**

**Figure 6** _(A sketch of the curve $C$ with parametric equations $x = 8\sin^2 t$, $y = 2\sin 2t + 3\sin t$ ($0 \leqslant t \leqslant \frac{\pi}{2}$), showing region $R$ bounded by $C$, the x-axis and the line $x = 4$)._


Figure 6 shows a sketch of the curve $C$ with parametric equations

$$x = 8\sin^2 t \quad y = 2\sin 2t + 3\sin t \quad 0 \leqslant t \leqslant \frac{\pi}{2}$$

The region $R$, shown shaded in Figure 6, is bounded by $C$, the $x$-axis and the line with equation $x = 4$.

**(a)** Show that the area of $R$ is given by

$$\int_{0}^{a} (8 - 8\cos 4t + 48\sin^2 t \cos t) \, \mathrm{d}t$$

where $a$ is a constant to be found. **(5)**

**(b)** Hence, using algebraic integration, find the exact area of $R$. **(4)**

**(Total for Question 16 is 9 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 16**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Find the upper limit $t_1$ corresponding to $x = 4$**
$4 = 8\sin^2 t \Rightarrow \sin^2 t = \frac{1}{2} \Rightarrow \sin t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \Rightarrow t = \frac{\pi}{4}$
So $a = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
- **[B1]** $a = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

**Step 2: Find $\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}$**
$x = 8\sin^2 t \Rightarrow \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = 16\sin t \cos t = 8(2\sin t \cos t) = 8\sin 2t$
- **[M1]** Correctly differentiates $x$ with respect to $t$.

**Step 3: Set up the integral for area $\int y \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} \, \mathrm{d}t$**
$$\text{Area} = \int_{0}^{\pi/4} (2\sin 2t + 3\sin t)(16\sin t \cos t) \, \mathrm{d}t$$
- **[M1]** Sets up the parametric area integral $\int y \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} \, \mathrm{d}t$ with correct limits.

**Step 4: Expand and simplify the integrand**
$$(2\sin 2t + 3\sin t)(16\sin t \cos t) = 32\sin 2t \sin t \cos t + 48\sin^2 t \cos t$$
Using $\sin 2t = 2\sin t \cos t$:
$$= 32(\sin 2t)\left(\frac{1}{2}\sin 2t\right) + 48\sin^2 t \cos t = 16\sin^2 2t + 48\sin^2 t \cos t$$
Using double angle formula $\sin^2 2t = \frac{1 - \cos 4t}{2}$:
$$= 16\left(\frac{1 - \cos 4t}{2}\right) + 48\sin^2 t \cos t = 8(1 - \cos 4t) + 48\sin^2 t \cos t = 8 - 8\cos 4t + 48\sin^2 t \cos t$$
- **[M1]** Uses appropriate double angle or product identities to simplify the integrand into the required form.
- **[A1]** Fully rigorous and correct derivation of the required integral expression.

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 5: Integrate each term with respect to $t$**
$$\int_{0}^{\pi/4} (8 - 8\cos 4t + 48\sin^2 t \cos t) \, \mathrm{d}t$$
- $\int 8 \, \mathrm{d}t = 8t$
- $\int -8\cos 4t \, \mathrm{d}t = -2\sin 4t$
- $\int 48\sin^2 t \cos t \, \mathrm{d}t = 16\sin^3 t$ (using reverse chain rule or substitution).
- **[M1]** Attempts to integrate all three terms (at least two correct).
- **[A1]** Fully correct integrated expression: $8t - 2\sin 4t + 16\sin^3 t$.

**Step 6: Substitute limits $\frac{\pi}{4}$ and $0$**
$$\left[ 8t - 2\sin 4t + 16\sin^3 t \right]_{0}^{\pi/4} = \left( 8\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) - 2\sin(\pi) + 16\left(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right)^3 \right) - (0 - 0 + 0)$$
$= 2\pi - 2(0) + 16\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^3 = 2\pi + 16\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\right) = 2\pi + \frac{16}{2\sqrt{2}} = 2\pi + 4\sqrt{2}$
- **[M1]** Correctly substitutes the upper and lower limits.
- **[A1]** Exact area $2\pi + 4\sqrt{2}$ (or equivalent).

### **Examiner Report 16**

The final question on the paper proved to be a challenge for many candidates and there were several blank responses seen. It is possible that this was due to time management. Some candidates attempted unsuccessfully to change to a Cartesian equation, suggesting that possibly parametric integration had not been covered.

In part (a), candidates who recognised the need to use parametric integration were usually able to differentiate correctly to find $\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}$, multiply this by $y$ and then use the double angle formulae to find an acceptable expression for $y \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}$. It was common for candidates to score the first two marks. The next two marks were more elusive, and it was common to see candidates perform a lot of onerous manipulation without making any significant progress. Those who were most likely to be successful were the ones who had an expression in terms of $2\sin 2t$ and then spotting the need to use the double angle formula to form a term in $\cos 4t$. For many candidates the mark for the upper limit was all that was achieved in part (a). However, some failed to achieve it as they left it as $4$ (value for $x$ not $t$) or $45$ (degrees).

It was good to see students who were unsuccessful at part (a) still attempting part (b), and in some cases scoring full marks. Most candidates were able to integrate the first two terms correctly, but a significant proportion were unable to integrate the third term to get an expression in $\sin^3 t$. The majority of correct solutions came from the use of the reverse chain rule for the final term. Those who attempted to use integration by substitution or by parts were usually unsuccessful. Candidates who had successfully completed the integration often went on to score full marks.
