### **Question 1**

Find

$$\int \left( \frac{2}{3}x^3 - 6\sqrt{x} + 1 \right) dx$$

giving your answer in its simplest form. **(4)**

### **Mark Scheme 1**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Write terms with indices**
$$\int \left( \frac{2}{3}x^3 - 6x^{\frac{1}{2}} + 1 \right) dx$$
- **[B1]** At least two terms integrated correctly, or correct index structure seen.

**Step 2: Perform integration term by term**
$$= \frac{2}{3} \left(\frac{x^4}{4}\right) - 6 \left(\frac{x^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\frac{3}{2}}\right) + x$$
- **[M1]** Raises the power of at least one term by 1 ($x^n \to x^{n+1}$).

**Step 3: Simplify coefficients and indices**
$$= \frac{1}{6}x^4 - 4x^{\frac{3}{2}} + x + c$$
- **[A1]** Any two terms correct and simplified.
- **[A1]** All terms correct and simplified, with $+ c$.

### **Examiner Report 1**

This was a very accessible question on integration to start the paper and most candidates were able to score well. Most candidates attempted to separate the fraction into two terms and achieved at least one correct index. The first term caused problems for some with errors such as $x^{\frac{5}{2}}$, $x^{\frac{3}{2}}$, $x^{\frac{1}{2}}$. A few ended up with three terms as they had added or subtracted $x^{-2}$. A few tried to integrate the numerator and denominator or wrote $(2\sqrt{x}-3)x^{-2}$ but did not expand the brackets and integrated both inside and outside separately. Those who had scored the first M1 usually were able to raise the power by one on at least one term with a correct index and score the next method mark. Several made errors when processing the indices and ended up subtracting by one on at least one of the terms. A few made errors with the coefficients. The most common mistake was to forget the $+c$.

### **Question 2**

**(i)** Show that $x^2 - 8x + 17 > 0$ for all real values of $x$. **(3)**

**(ii)** "If I add $3$ to a number and square the sum, the result is greater than the square of the original number."

State, giving a reason, if the above statement is always true, sometimes true or never true. **(2)**

**(Total for Question 2 is 5 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 2**

#### **Part (i)**

**Step 1: Complete the square**
$x^2 - 8x + 17 \equiv (x - 4)^2 - 16 + 17 \equiv (x - 4)^2 + 1$
- **[M1]** Attempt to complete the square, achieving $(x \pm 4)^2 + \dots$

**Step 2: Deduce positivity**
Since $(x - 4)^2 \ge 0$ for all real $x$, then $(x - 4)^2 + 1 \ge 1 > 0$.
- **[A1]** Correct completed form $(x - 4)^2 + 1$.
- **[A1]** Complete valid argument showing $(x - 4)^2 + 1 > 0$ for all real $x$ (or discriminant method $b^2 - 4ac = 64 - 68 = -4 < 0$ and $a > 0$).

#### **Part (ii)**

**Step 1: Formulate the algebraic expression**
Let the number be $x$. $(x + 3)^2 = x^2 + 6x + 9$. Compare with $x^2$.
Difference is $(x + 3)^2 - x^2 = 6x + 9$.

**Step 2: State truth value and give counterexample or reason**
Sometimes true. For example, if $x = -2$, $(-2 + 3)^2 = 1$, but $(-2)^2 = 4$, and $1$ is not greater than $4$. (Or working with inequalities $6x + 9 > 0 \Rightarrow x > -1.5$, so it is only true for $x > -1.5$).
- **[B1]** States "sometimes true" (or equivalent valid explanation).
- **[B1]** Gives a valid reason or counterexample (e.g., trying a negative number $\le -1.5$).

### **Examiner Report 2**

This question tested the ability to complete the square and evaluate mathematical statements. In part (i), most candidates successfully completed the square to obtain $(x - 4)^2 + 1$ and deduced that since $(x - 4)^2 \ge 0$, the expression must be greater than zero. A small number used the discriminant method successfully. In part (ii), many candidates correctly identified that the statement was sometimes true, providing a counterexample such as $x = -2$, though some struggled to articulate the conditions under which it held.

### **Question 3**

Given that the point $A$ has position vector $4\mathbf{i} - 5\mathbf{j}$ and the point $B$ has position vector $-5\mathbf{i} - 2\mathbf{j}$,

**(a)** find the vector $\overrightarrow{AB}$, **(2)**

**(b)** find $|\overrightarrow{AB}|$.

Give your answer as a simplified surd. **(2)**

**(Total for Question 3 is 4 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 3**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Calculate $\overrightarrow{AB}$**
$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a} = (-5\mathbf{i} - 2\mathbf{j}) - (4\mathbf{i} - 5\mathbf{j}) = -9\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j}$$
- **[M1]** Attempt $\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$ or $\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$ with at least one correct component.
- **[A1]** Correct vector $-9\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j}$ (or $\binom{-9}{3}$).

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 1: Calculate magnitude**
$$|\overrightarrow{AB}| = \sqrt{(-9)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{81 + 9} = \sqrt{90}$$
- **[M1]** Correct use of Pythagoras' theorem on their vector components.

**Step 2: Simplify surd**
$$\sqrt{90} = \sqrt{9 \times 10} = 3\sqrt{10}$$
- **[A1]** Correct simplified surd $3\sqrt{10}$.

### **Examiner Report 3**

Part (a) was answered well by the majority of candidates who correctly subtracted the position vector of $A$ from that of $B$. The most common error was subtracting in the reverse order or making a sign slip. In part (b), most candidates applied Pythagoras' theorem correctly to their vector from part (a), though a few failed to give their answer as a fully simplified surd, leaving $\sqrt{90}$.

### **Question 4**

The line $l_1$ has equation $4y - 3x = 10$

The line $l_2$ passes through the points $(5, -1)$ and $(-1, 8)$.

Determine, giving full reasons for your answer, whether lines $l_1$ and $l_2$ are parallel, perpendicular or neither. **(4)**

**(Total for Question 4 is 4 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 4**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Find gradient of $l_1$**
$$4y = 3x + 10 \implies y = \frac{3}{4}x + \frac{5}{2}$$
Gradient of $l_1 = \frac{3}{4}$
- **[B1]** Correct gradient of $l_1$ (i.e. $\frac{3}{4}$).

**Step 2: Find gradient of $l_2$**
$$\text{Gradient of } l_2 = \frac{8 - (-1)}{-1 - 5} = \frac{9}{-6} = -\frac{3}{2}$$
- **[M1]** Correct formula and attempt to find the gradient of $l_2$.
- **[A1]** Correct gradient of $l_2$ (i.e. $-\frac{3}{2}$).

**Step 3: Compare gradients and conclude**
$\frac{3}{4} \neq -\frac{3}{2}$ (not parallel) and $\frac{3}{4} \times -\frac{3}{2} = -\frac{9}{8} \neq -1$ (not perpendicular). Therefore, neither.
- **[A1]** Correct conclusion supported by valid reasoning comparing gradients.

### **Examiner Report 4**

Candidates generally demonstrated a good understanding of how to find the gradient of a straight line from both its equation and from two points. Most successfully found the gradient of $l_1$ to be $\frac{3}{4}$ and the gradient of $l_2$ to be $-\frac{3}{2}$. A common error was failing to state a clear conclusion or omitting the check for perpendicularity, though the majority correctly deduced that the lines were neither parallel nor perpendicular.

### **Question 5**

A student's attempt to solve the equation $2\log_2 x - \log_2 \sqrt{x} = 3$ is shown below.

$$2\log_2 x - \log_2 \sqrt{x} = 3$$

$$2\log_2 \left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{x}}\right) = 3 \quad \text{using the subtraction law for logs}$$

$$2\log_2 (\sqrt{x}) = 3 \quad \text{simplifying}$$

$$\log_2 x = 3 \quad \text{using the power law for logs}$$

$$x = 3^2 = 9 \quad \text{using the definition of a log}$$

**(a)** Identify two errors made by this student, giving a brief explanation of each. **(2)**

**(b)** Write out the correct solution. **(3)**

**(Total for Question 5 is 5 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 5**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Identify first error**
Error 1: Applying the subtraction law inside the coefficient of 2 (i.e. $2(\log A - \log B) \neq 2\log(\frac{A}{B})$ unless the 2 is brought inside first, or subtracting before applying the power law).
- **[B1]** Identifies an error in the first step (e.g., the 2 should be applied as a power first, or subtraction rule applied incorrectly with the factor of 2).

**Step 2: Identify second error**
Error 2: In the final step, converting $\log_2 x = 3$ to $x = 3^2$ instead of $x = 2^3$.
- **[B1]** Identifies the index/base error in the final step ($2^3$ used as $3^2$).

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 1: Apply log laws correctly**
$$2\log_2 x - \log_2(x^{\frac{1}{2}}) = 3$$
$$\log_2(x^2) - \log_2(x^{\frac{1}{2}}) = 3$$
$$\log_2\left(\frac{x^2}{x^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right) = 3$$
$$\log_2(x^{\frac{3}{2}}) = 3$$
- **[M1]** Uses power law to get $\log_2(x^2)$ and subtraction law correctly to get $\log_2\left(\frac{x^2}{\sqrt{x}}\right) = 3$.

**Step 2: Solve for $x$**
$$x^{\frac{3}{2}} = 2^3 = 8$$
- **[M1]** Correctly rewrites logarithmic equation as an index equation $x^{\frac{3}{2}} = 2^3$ (or equivalent).

**Step 3: Evaluate $x$**
$$x = 8^{\frac{2}{3}} = (2^3)^{\frac{2}{3}} = 4$$
- **[A1]** $x = 4$.

### **Examiner Report 5**

Part (a) was well answered by candidates who spotted both that the coefficient of 2 could not simply remain outside when applying the subtraction law and that the base and argument were swapped in the final exponential step ($3^2$ instead of $2^3$). In part (b), candidates who correctly applied the power law first followed by the subtraction law were generally successful in reaching $x^{\frac{3}{2}} = 8$, though many struggled to evaluate $8^{\frac{2}{3}}$ correctly without a calculator or made arithmetic slips.

### **Question 7**

In a triangle $ABC$, side $AB$ has length $10\text{ cm}$, side $AC$ has length $5\text{ cm}$, and angle $BAC = \theta$ where $\theta$ is measured in degrees. The area of triangle $ABC$ is $15\text{ cm}^2$.

**(a)** Find the two possible values of $\cos \theta$. **(4)**

Given that $BC$ is the longest side of the triangle,

**(b)** find the exact length of $BC$. **(2)**

**(Total for Question 7 is 6 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 7**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Use area formula**
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}ab \sin C \implies \frac{1}{2}(10)(5)\sin \theta = 15$$
$$25\sin \theta = 15 \implies \sin \theta = \frac{15}{25} = \frac{3}{5}$$
- **[M1]** Uses the correct area formula with given values.
- **[A1]** $\sin \theta = \frac{3}{5}$.

**Step 2: Find possible values of $\cos \theta$**
$$\cos^2 \theta = 1 - \sin^2 \theta = 1 - \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 = \frac{16}{25}$$
$$\cos \theta = \pm \frac{4}{5}$$
- **[M1]** Uses $\cos^2 \theta + \sin^2 \theta = 1$ to find $\cos \theta$.
- **[A1]** $\cos \theta = \frac{4}{5}$ and $\cos \theta = -\frac{4}{5}$.

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 1: Use cosine rule with correct $\theta$**
Since $BC$ is the longest side, angle $BAC$ must be obtuse, so $\cos \theta = -\frac{4}{5}$.
$$BC^2 = 10^2 + 5^2 - 2(10)(5)\cos \theta$$
$$BC^2 = 100 + 25 - 100\left(-\frac{4}{5}\right) = 125 + 80 = 205$$
- **[M1]** Selects the negative value of $\cos \theta$ and applies the cosine rule correctly.
- **[A1]** $BC = \sqrt{205}$.

### **Examiner Report 7**

In part (a), most candidates successfully used the area formula $\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$ to find $\sin \theta = \frac{3}{5}$. Many then correctly found both positive and negative values for $\cos \theta$ using $\cos^2 \theta = 1 - \sin^2 \theta$. In part (b), candidates needed to select the negative value of $\cos \theta$ because the longest side corresponds to the obtuse angle. While many recognized this, a number of candidates used the positive value or guessed incorrectly.

### **Question 8**

A lorry is driven between London and Newcastle.

In a simple model, the cost of the journey $£C$ when the lorry is driven at a steady speed of $v$ kilometres per hour is

$$C = \frac{1500}{v} + \frac{2}{11}v + 60$$

**(a)** Find, according to this model,

**(i)** the value of $v$ that minimises the cost of the journey,

**(ii)** the minimum cost of the journey.

(Solutions based entirely on graphical or numerical methods are not acceptable.) **(6)**

**(b)** Prove by using $\frac{d^2C}{dv^2}$ that the cost is minimised at the speed found in (a)(i). **(2)**

**(c)** State one limitation of this model. **(1)**

**(Total for Question 8 is 9 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 8**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Differentiate $C$ with respect to $v$**
$$\frac{dC}{dv} = -1500v^{-2} + \frac{2}{11} = -\frac{1500}{v^2} + \frac{2}{11}$$
- **[M1]** Attempts to differentiate $C$, with at least one non-zero term correct.
- **[A1]** Correct derivative $\frac{dC}{dv} = -\frac{1500}{v^2} + \frac{2}{11}$.

**Step 2: Set derivative to zero and solve for $v$**
$$-\frac{1500}{v^2} + \frac{2}{11} = 0 \implies \frac{2}{11} = \frac{1500}{v^2}$$
$$2v^2 = 16500 \implies v^2 = 8250 \implies v = \sqrt{8250} \approx 90.8$$
- **[M1]** Equates derivative to zero and rearranges to solve for $v^2$ or $v$.
- **[A1]** $v = \sqrt{8250}$ or $55\sqrt{6}$ or $90.8$.

**Step 3: Calculate minimum cost**
$$C = \frac{1500}{90.829} + \frac{2}{11}(90.829) + 60 \approx 165.15 + 16.51 + 60 = 241.66$$
- **[A1]** Minimum cost $= £242$ (or exact $60 + 20\sqrt{825}$).

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 1: Find second derivative**
$$\frac{d^2C}{dv^2} = 3000v^{-3} = \frac{3000}{v^3}$$
- **[M1]** Attempts second derivative.

**Step 2: Evaluate and conclude**
When $v = \sqrt{8250} > 0$, $\frac{d^2C}{dv^2} > 0$, therefore it is a minimum.
- **[A1]** Evaluates $\frac{d^2C}{dv^2} > 0$ (or substitutes $v$ to get $\approx 0.0036 > 0$) with a valid conclusion.

#### **Part (c)**

**Step 1: State limitation**
The model does not account for traffic, variable speed, rest breaks, road conditions, fuel price changes, etc.
- **[B1]** Any sensible real-world limitation regarding lorry driving (e.g., speed limits, traffic, terrain).

### **Examiner Report 8**

Part (a) was well answered by candidates who differentiated the expression correctly, set it to zero, and solved for $v$. Most candidates found the minimum cost correctly by substituting their value of $v$ back into the original equation. In part (b), finding the second derivative and substituting $v$ to show it was positive was handled competently by the majority. Part (c) elicited a wide variety of sensible real-world limitations, which were generally awarded the mark.

### **Question 10**

Prove, from first principles, that the derivative of $x^3$ is $3x^2$. **(4)**

**(Total for Question 10 is 4 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 10**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Set up limit definition**
$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x + h)^3 - x^3}{h}$$
- **[B1]** States the correct first principles formula: $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ with $f(x) = x^3$.

**Step 2: Expand $(x + h)^3$**
$$(x + h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3$$
- **[M1]** Expands $(x + h)^3$ correctly.

**Step 3: Simplify numerator**
$$\frac{x^3 + 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3 - x^3}{h} = \frac{3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3}{h} = 3x^2 + 3xh + h^2$$
- **[A1]** Simplifies the algebraic fraction to $3x^2 + 3xh + h^2$.

**Step 4: Take limit as $h \to 0$**
As $h \to 0$, $3xh + h^2 \to 0$, leaving $3x^2$. Hence $\frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2$.
- **[A1]** Completes the proof by taking the limit as $h \to 0$ and providing a clear concluding statement.

### **Examiner Report 10**

This question was generally well answered. Most candidates knew the correct definition of differentiation from first principles. Errors typically occurred during the expansion of $(x + h)^3$, where coefficients were sometimes incorrect, or in algebraic cancellation when dividing by $h$. A small number of candidates failed to explicitly state that they were taking the limit as $h \to 0$ at the end of the proof.

### **Question 11**

**(a)** Find the first 3 terms, in ascending powers of $x$, of the binomial expansion of

$$\left(2 - \frac{x}{16}\right)^9$$

$$\text{f}(x) = (a + bx)\left(2 - \frac{x}{16}\right)^9, \quad \text{where } a \text{ and } b \text{ are constants}$$

**(c)** find the value of $b$. **(2)**

### **Mark Scheme 11**

- **[A1]** First term correct ($512$).
- **[A1]** Second term correct ($-144x$).
- **[A1]** Third term correct ($18x^2$).

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 1: Multiply $(a + bx)$ by the expansion**

- **[M1]** Multiplies $(a + bx)$ by their first term (or first two terms) of the expansion.

**Step 2: Equate constant term to $128$**

**Step 1: Equate coefficient of $x$ to $36$**
$$-144a + 512b = 36$$
- **[M1]** Equates the coefficient of $x$ from the product to $36$.

**Step 2: Solve for $b$**
$$-144\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + 512b = 36$$
$$-36 + 512b = 36 \implies 512b = 72 \implies b = \frac{72}{512} = \frac{9}{64}$$
- **[A1]** $b = \frac{9}{64}$ (or $0.140625$).

### **Examiner Report 11**

Part (a) was answered well by many candidates who successfully applied the binomial expansion formula, though errors with signs and powers of 16 were common. In part (b), candidates generally understood that multiplying $(a + bx)$ by their expansion and equating the constant term to 128 would yield $a$, which most completed successfully. In part (c), equating the coefficient of $x$ to 36 and substituting their value of $a$ allowed successful candidates to determine $b$, though arithmetic slips were occasionally penalised.

### **Question 12**

**(a)** Show that the equation

$$4\cos \theta - 1 = 2\sin \theta \tan \theta$$

can be written in the form

$$6\cos^2 \theta - \cos \theta - 2 = 0$$ **(4)**

**(b)** Hence solve, for $0 \le x < 90^\circ$,

$$4\cos 3x - 1 = 2\sin 3x \tan 3x$$

giving your answers, where appropriate, to one decimal place.

(Solutions based entirely on graphical or numerical methods are not acceptable.) **(4)**

**(Total for Question 12 is 8 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 12**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Substitute $\tan \theta = \frac{\sin \theta}{\cos \theta}$**
$$4\cos \theta - 1 = 2\sin \theta \left(\frac{\sin \theta}{\cos \theta}\right)$$
- **[M1]** Uses $\tan \theta = \frac{\sin \theta}{\cos \theta}$.

**Step 2: Multiply through by $\cos \theta$**
$$4\cos^2 \theta - \cos \theta = 2\sin^2 \theta$$
- **[M1]** Clears the denominator by multiplying by $\cos \theta$.

**Step 3: Replace $\sin^2 \theta$ with $1 - \cos^2 \theta$**
$$4\cos^2 \theta - \cos \theta = 2(1 - \cos^2 \theta)$$
$$4\cos^2 \theta - \cos \theta = 2 - 2\cos^2 \theta$$
- **[M1]** Uses identity $\sin^2 \theta = 1 - \cos^2 \theta$.

**Step 4: Rearrange into required quadratic form**
$$6\cos^2 \theta - \cos \theta - 2 = 0$$
- **[A1]** Obtains the given printed quadratic equation with no errors seen.

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 1: Solve quadratic for $\cos 3x$**
$$(3\cos 3x - 2)(2\cos 3x + 1) = 0$$
$$\cos 3x = \frac{2}{3} \quad \text{or} \quad \cos 3x = -\frac{1}{2}$$
- **[M1]** Solves the quadratic to find values for $\cos 3x$.

**Step 2: Find values for $3x$**
For $\cos 3x = \frac{2}{3}$:
$$3x = 48.189^\circ, 311.811^\circ, 408.189^\circ, \dots$$
For $\cos 3x = -\frac{1}{2}$:
$$3x = 120^\circ, 240^\circ, 480^\circ, \dots$$
- **[M1]** Finds correct angles for $3x$ within range $0 \le 3x < 270^\circ$.

**Step 3: Divide by 3 to find $x$**
$$x = 16.1^\circ, 40.0^\circ, 80.3^\circ$$
- **[A1]** $16.1^\circ, 40.0^\circ, 80.3^\circ$ (all three correct, rounding to 1 decimal place).

### **Examiner Report 12**

In part (a), many candidates successfully substituted $\tan \theta = \frac{\sin \theta}{\cos \theta}$ and used $\sin^2 \theta = 1 - \cos^2 \theta$ to arrive at the required quadratic, though algebraic slips were occasionally seen. In part (b), candidates who correctly solved the quadratic for $\cos 3x$ often missed some solutions by not extending the range for $3x$ ($0 \le 3x < 270^\circ$) before dividing by 3. Candidates should be reminded to adjust their intervals carefully when solving equations involving multiple angles.

### **Question 13**

**Figure 3** _(A sketch of a straight line graph of $\log_{10} V$ against $t$, with positive gradient and intercept with the vertical axis at $\log_{10} p$, labelled as line l)._

```tikz
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9]
  \draw[->] (-0.5,0) -- (4,0) node[below] {$t$};
  \draw[->] (0,-0.5) -- (0,4) node[left] {$\log_{10} V$};
```

The value of a rare painting, $£V$, is modelled by the equation $V = pq^t$, where $p$ and $q$ are constants and $t$ is the number of years since the value of the painting was first recorded on 1st January 1980.

The line $l$ shown in Figure 3 illustrates the linear relationship between $t$ and $\log_{10} V$ since 1st January 1980.

The equation of line $l$ is $\log_{10} V = 0.05t + 4.8$

**(a)** Find, to 4 significant figures, the value of $p$ and the value of $q$. **(4)**

**(b)** With reference to the model interpret

**(i)** the value of the constant $p$,

**(ii)** the value of the constant $q$. **(2)**

**(c)** Find the value of the painting, as predicted by the model, on 1st January 2010, giving your answer to the nearest hundred thousand pounds. **(2)**

**(Total for Question 13 is 8 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 13**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Take logarithms of $V = pq^t$**
$$\log_{10} V = \log_{10} (pq^t) = \log_{10} p + t \log_{10} q$$
- **[M1]** Takes $\log_{10}$ of $V = pq^t$ and uses log laws to relate to the linear form $y = mx + c$.

**Step 2: Equate intercept to find $p$**
$$\log_{10} p = 4.8 \implies p = 10^{4.8} = 63100$$
- **[A1]** $p = 63100$ (or $6.310 \times 10^4$).

**Step 3: Equate gradient to find $q$**
$$\log_{10} q = 0.05 \implies q = 10^{0.05} = 1.122$$
- **[A1]** $q = 1.122$ (to 4 s.f.).

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 1: Interpret $p$**
**(i)** $p$ is the initial value of the painting in pounds (on 1st January 1980 when $t = 0$).
- **[B1]** Correct interpretation of $p$ (initial value/value when $t=0$).

**Step 2: Interpret $q$**
**(ii)** $q$ is the annual growth factor of the value of the painting (or the multiplier by which the value increases each year).
- **[B1]** Correct interpretation of $q$ (growth factor/multiplier per year).

#### **Part (c)**

**Step 1: Determine $t$ for 1st January 2010**
$2010 - 1980 = 30$ years, so $t = 30$.
- **[M1]** Substitutes $t = 30$ into the model equation.

**Step 2: Calculate $V$**
$$\log_{10} V = 0.05(30) + 4.8 = 1.5 + 4.8 = 6.3$$
$$V = 10^{6.3} = 1\,995\,262$$
To nearest hundred thousand pounds: $2\,000\,000$.
- **[A1]** $2\,000\,000$ (or $£2\,000\,000$).

### **Examiner Report 13**

Part (a) was well answered by candidates who recognised that taking logarithms of $V = pq^t$ gives $\log_{10} V = t \log_{10} q + \log_{10} p$, allowing them to equate the gradient and intercept to the given linear equation. In part (b), many candidates gave vague interpretations for $p$ and $q$, though better candidates clearly stated that $p$ was the initial value and $q$ was the annual multiplier. Part (c) was well done by those who correctly identified $t = 30$ and rounded to the nearest hundred thousand pounds as requested.

### **Question 14**

The circle $C$ has equation

$$x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 10y + 9 = 0$$

**(a)** Find

**(i)** the coordinates of the centre of $C$,

**(ii)** the radius of $C$. **(3)**

The line with equation $y = kx$, where $k$ is a constant, cuts $C$ at two distinct points.

**(b)** Find the range of values for $k$. **(6)**

**(Total for Question 14 is 9 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 14**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Complete the square for $x$ and $y$**
$$(x - 3)^2 - 9 + (y + 5)^2 - 25 + 9 = 0$$
$$(x - 3)^2 + (y + 5)^2 = 25$$
- **[M1]** Attempts to complete the square for both $x$ and $y$.

**Step 2: State centre and radius**
**(i)** Centre = $(3, -5)$.
**(ii)** Radius = $5$.
- **[A1]** Centre $(3, -5)$.
- **[A1]** Radius $5$.

#### **Part (b)**

**Step 1: Substitute $y = kx$ into the circle equation**
$x^2 + (kx)^2 - 6x + 10(kx) + 9 = 0$
$$(1 + k^2)x^2 + (10k - 6)x + 9 = 0$$
- **[M1]** Substitutes $y = kx$ into the circle equation and collects terms into quadratic form in $x$.

**Step 2: Use condition for two distinct points ($\Delta > 0$)**
$$b^2 - 4ac > 0$$
$$(10k - 6)^2 - 4(1 + k^2)(9) > 0$$
$$100k^2 - 120k + 36 - 36 - 36k^2 > 0$$
$$64k^2 - 120k > 0$$
- **[M1]** Sets discriminant $b^2 - 4ac > 0$ for the quadratic.
- **[A1]** Correct inequality: $64k^2 - 120k > 0$ (or equivalent simplified quadratic inequality).

**Step 3: Solve quadratic inequality**
$$8k(8k - 15) > 0 \implies k < 0 \quad \text{or} \quad k > \frac{15}{8}$$
- **[M1]** Finds critical values ($0$ and $\frac{15}{8}$) and selects the exterior regions.
- **[A1]** $k < 0 \text{ or } k > \frac{15}{8}$ (or set notation $\{k : k < 0\} \cup \{k : k > \frac{15}{8}\}$).

### **Examiner Report 14**

In part (a), most candidates successfully completed the square to find the centre $(3, -5)$ and radius $5$. In part (b), substituting $y = kx$ into the circle equation and setting the discriminant greater than zero was well understood, though algebraic expansion errors in simplifying $(10k - 6)^2 - 36(1 + k^2)$ were common. Solving the resulting quadratic inequality correctly to find the two ranges for $k$ was a good discriminator for higher-scoring candidates.

### **Question 15**

**Figure 4** _(A sketch of part of the curve $C$ with equation $y = \frac{32}{x^2} + 3x - 8$, showing region $R$ bounded by $C$, the normal $l$ at $P(4, 6)$, the line $x = 2$, and the x-axis)._

```tikz
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9]
  \draw[->] (-0.5,0) -- (6,0) node[below] {$x$};
  \draw[->] (0,-1) -- (0,5) node[left] {$y$};
  \draw[domain=1.5:5.5, smooth, thick] plot (\x, {32/(\x*\x) + 3*(\x) - 8});
  \draw[thick] (2,0) -- (4,6) -- (5.2,0);
  \fill (4,6) circle (1.6pt) node[above right] {$P(4, 6)$};
  \node at (3,2) {$R$};
\end{tikzpicture}
```

Figure 4 shows a sketch of part of the curve $C$ with equation

$$y = \frac{32}{x^2} + 3x - 8, \quad x > 0$$

The point $P(4, 6)$ lies on $C$.

The line $l$ is the normal to $C$ at the point $P$.

The region $R$, shown shaded in Figure 4, is bounded by the line $l$, the curve $C$, the line with equation $x = 2$ and the $x$-axis.

Show that the area of $R$ is $46$.

(Solutions based entirely on graphical or numerical methods are not acceptable.) **(10)**

**(Total for Question 15 is 10 marks)**

### **Mark Scheme 15**

#### **Part (a)**

**Step 1: Differentiate to find gradient of curve at $P$**
$$y = 32x^{-2} + 3x - 8 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = -64x^{-3} + 3 = -\frac{64}{x^3} + 3$$
- **[M1]** Attempts to differentiate $y$ (at least one term correct power).
- **[A1]** Correct derivative $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{64}{x^3} + 3$.

**Step 2: Find gradient of curve and normal at $x = 4$**
$$\text{Gradient of curve} = -\frac{64}{4^3} + 3 = -\frac{64}{64} + 3 = -1 + 3 = 2$$
$$\text{Gradient of normal} = -\frac{1}{2}$$
- **[M1]** Substitutes $x = 4$ into derivative to find gradient of tangent, and uses $m_1 m_2 = -1$ to find gradient of normal.

**Step 3: Find equation of normal $l$**
$$y - 6 = -\frac{1}{2}(x - 4) \implies y = -\frac{1}{2}x + 8$$
- **[M1]** Finds equation of normal using $(4, 6)$ and their gradient.

**Step 4: Find $x$-intercept of normal $l$**
$$0 = -\frac{1}{2}x + 8 \implies x = 16$$
- **[A1]** Identifies $x$-intercept of normal at $(16, 0)$ (or triangle base/height).

**Step 5: Integrate curve between limits $x = 2$ and $x = 4$**
$$\int_2^4 \left(32x^{-2} + 3x - 8\right) dx = \left[-32x^{-1} + \frac{3}{2}x^2 - 8x\right]_2^4$$
$$= \left(-\frac{32}{4} + \frac{3}{2}(16) - 8(4)\right) - \left(-\frac{32}{2} + \frac{3}{2}(4) - 8(2)\right)$$
$$= (-8 + 24 - 32) - (-16 + 6 - 16) = -16 - (-26) = 10$$
- **[M1]** Integrates the curve function correctly.
- **[A1]** Correct integration expression with limits evaluated to $10$.

**Step 6: Calculate area of the triangle under the normal**
$$\text{Area of triangle} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} \times (16 - 4) \times 6 = \frac{1}{2} \times 12 \times 6 = 36$$
(Alternatively, integrate normal line from $4$ to $16$: $\int_4^{16} (-\frac{1}{2}x + 8) dx = 36$).
- **[M1]** Calculates area of the region under the normal line (via geometry or integration).

**Step 7: Combine areas**
$$\text{Total area} = 10 + 36 = 46$$
- **[A1]** Concludes that the total area is $46$ with full valid working.

### **Examiner Report 15**

This multi-step calculus question required candidates to find the equation of the normal, integrate the curve between limits, and combine the resulting areas. Most candidates successfully differentiated the curve, found the gradient at $x = 4$, and determined the equation of the normal. Integration of the curve between 2 and 4 was generally well done. The final steps—calculating the area of the triangle under the normal and summing the two areas to get 46—were completed successfully by well-prepared candidates, though arithmetic errors and confusion over limits were occasional pitfalls.
